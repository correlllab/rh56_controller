Simulation study · RH56 grasp strategies

Approach and closure admissibility for three RH56 grasp strategies

A geometric feasibility model for the commanded motion of naive, iterative and thumb-reflex closure, run over the same 15 objects and 10 approach conditions as the hardware benchmark.

Date 2026-09-08 Status simulation only, no hardware Trials {{N_TRIALS}} per variant Model {{XML_NAME}}

§1 Why this experiment exists

Reviewer 15 of the IROS submission accepted the hardware results and rejected the reasoning around them: “All elements are selected empirically, and even if each step sounds reasonable, it does not provide a clear assessment that the strategy will correctly operate.” The three closure strategies were designed by hand, ranked by a 450-trial benchmark, and never shown to follow from the hand's geometry. The author response promised a simulation analysis of the reachability of each strategy as a function of object shape and volume and of initial hand pose.

That analysis is what this page reports. The question it answers is narrow and geometric: for a given object on a table and a given starting hand pose, can each strategy execute the motion it commands without striking the support surface, without hitting the object before the grasp closes, and with its contacts ending up on the object? Grasp success is not predicted here, and no dynamics, friction or object compliance are modelled.

An earlier attempt at this analysis exists in the repository and does not answer the question. tools/run_strategy_pregrasp_rate.py checks whether the hand can translate to each strategy's pre-grasp pose with the fingers held in a fixed shape. Naive and reflex closure both approach with the opposing fingers open and both target the analytical final pose, so that check admits them at nearly identical rates and cannot separate the strategies at all. On the sugar box it returns 100% for naive and 100% for reflex. The discriminating phase is closure, which that tool never simulates.

§2 The mechanism under test

The RH56 finger linkages are coupled, so a commanded width sets fingertip position and the hand pose needed to hold a grasp plane at the same time. Sweeping the analytical solver across its width range gives the size of the effect directly. In four-finger plane mode the hand tilt runs from {{TILT_WIDE}}° at the widest solvable width ({{W_MAX}} mm) to 90° at the narrowest ({{W_MIN}} mm), and the antipodal contact midpoint moves {{CENTER_TRAVEL}} mm within the hand frame over that range.

A parallel-jaw gripper has neither property: its fingertips converge on the aperture midpoint and the wrist pose that was correct with the jaws open stays correct as they close. Any strategy that treats this hand as a parallel jaw therefore inherits an uncompensated pose error equal to that travel, and the error is largest exactly where clearance above the table is smallest.

§3 What each strategy commands

Each strategy is modelled as an ordered sequence of waypoints in hand-base position, hand-base rotation, and the six actuator commands, following Sec. IV of the manuscript. The three differ only in what the wrist does while the fingers close.

Naive closure

Wrist fixed at the open-hand pose

The fully open hand is placed so its antipodal midpoint sits on the grasp target, as a parallel-jaw pipeline would, and the width command is then applied with the wrist held still. The whole coupled fingertip travel is uncompensated.

Iterative closure

Wrist tracks the width sequence

The hand starts at the widest analytical pose and steps down in {{ITER_STEP}} mm width increments. Wrist pose and actuator command move together, so the grasp plane is held at every step and the terminal contact is exact.

Thumb reflex

Wrist fixed at the final pose

The thumb is placed at its final bend and opposition while the other active fingers stay open, with the wrist already at the final analytical pose. Only the opposing fingers move during closure, which is the smallest swept volume of the three.

Every strategy is released from the same world hand-base position, because the arm commands the wrist and all three start from the same arm state. Each then holds its own pre-grasp orientation and finger shape, which is the difference under test.

§4 Hypotheses

Three predictions follow from the coupling, and each can fail against the model.

  • H1. Naive closure loses admissibility first as the grasp point approaches the table, because the uncompensated fingertip travel is directed into the support surface. Falsified if naive's admissible fraction is independent of grasp-point height.
  • H2. Naive closure alone produces terminal contact misalignment, of the order of the fingertip travel in §2. Falsified if iterative or reflex closure also miss their targets, or if naive's offset is small compared with object size.
  • H3. Reflex closure retains more of the low-clearance region than iterative closure, because it holds the opposing fingers extended and moves the wrist once. Falsified if reflex incurs at least as many support-surface collisions as iterative.

§5 Admissibility criteria

The hand is checked against the object proxy and against the support plane at {{N_SAMPLES}} sampled configurations along each commanded motion. Object contact uses the 13-capsule proxy already in the repository; the support plane uses the model's own collision geometry, so that test needs no tolerance. A trial is admissible when none of three failure modes fires.

table_collision
Any part of the hand's collision geometry — palm boxes and cylinder, phalanx meshes — falls below the support plane at any sampled configuration. This test carries no clearance tolerance. §7 measures what a tolerance would have to be if the faster capsule proxy were used instead.
premature_object_contact
Any part of the hand other than the planned contact fingertips penetrates the object. The distal capsules of the thumb and of the opposing fingers named by the analytical solution are exempt, since those are the grasp contacts; the palm, the inactive fingers and the proximal phalanges are not.
terminal_misalignment
The antipodal contact midpoint actually reached at the end of the motion lies outside the object proxy, so the commanded closure no longer straddles the object.

The planned grasp width is the object width plus {{WIDTH_OFFSET}} mm, matching the planner, so a correctly executed grasp leaves roughly {{HALF_OFFSET}} mm of clearance per side and any contact in this model is unintended.

H1 — support-surface collision

Naive closure records {{FAIL_TABLE_NAIVE}} support-surface collisions of 150 trials, against {{FAIL_TABLE_ITER}} for iterative and {{FAIL_TABLE_REFLEX}} for reflex. Its admissible fraction is a step in grasp-point height, not a gradient: zero for all {{N_LOW_OBJECTS}} objects with a grasp point at or below {{NAIVE_ZERO_MAX}} mm, and 9 of 10 for all {{N_HIGH_OBJECTS}} objects at or above {{NAIVE_OK_MIN}} mm. Its rate is also identical at both elevated approach heights, 53.3% at 150 mm and at 250 mm, so the mechanism is set by the object rather than by the start pose. The prediction holds.

H2 — terminal contact misalignment

Naive closure displaces the terminal contact midpoint by a median {{NAIVE_OFFSET_MED}} mm, range {{NAIVE_OFFSET_MIN}} to {{NAIVE_OFFSET_MAX}} mm, which is the same order as the in-hand contact travel measured in §2. Iterative and reflex closure reach their targets to within {{OTHER_OFFSET_MAX}} mm, exactly as the analytical construction requires. For the six objects whose grasp width is 25 mm or less, the displacement carries the contact midpoint outside the object and the grasp misses it entirely, at every one of the ten approach conditions: {{FAIL_TERM_NAIVE}} of the 150 trials. The prediction holds.

H3 — clearance during approach

Reflex closure records {{FAIL_TABLE_REFLEX}} support-surface collisions against {{FAIL_TABLE_ITER}} for iterative, and it is the only strategy that admits any trial at P1, the condition level with the grasp point that the manuscript calls adversarial ({{P1_REFLEX}} of 15, against {{P1_ITER}} and {{P1_NAIVE}}). The advantage is confined to the low approach conditions: at 150 mm the two differ 100% to 80%, and at 250 mm both admit every trial. The prediction holds in the region it was made for.

The start poses each strategy cannot reach from

The condition set samples ten start poses per object. Sweeping the start pose instead over the region an arm is actually commanded from — the quarter ball of radius 300 mm above the grasp point and on the approach side — gives the set of poses each strategy cannot execute from. Every cell is evaluated independently rather than scanning each column down to its lowest admissible height, so the result can represent overhangs; over five object and grasp-height combinations no column was non-monotone in height, which is a property of the problem rather than an assumption of the model. §7 Calibrating the model against the hardware

An earlier version of this page recorded the opposite of the result above: the two planned strategies saturated at 65% and 78% however the collision proxy was loosened, the residual failures were 30–60 mm deep, and the conclusion was that the gap could not be closed by tuning. That reading was right about the proxy and wrong about the cause. The gap was in the model's inputs.

Object cross-sections. The proxies were first-pass estimates. Measured values for six objects: raspberry 20 mm (unchanged), small screwdriver 22→15, pen 12→15, charger 60→35, big screwdriver 35→25, strawberry 30→25. Five of the six shrink, which lowers their grasp points and moves two more objects below the height at which the naive terminal pose misses the object entirely.

Small screwdriver handle height. Taking the 15 mm as the whole proxy put the screwdriver's grasp point at 7.5 mm and took it to 0/10 on every strategy, against 0/5/9 for the raspberry at the same 20 mm class. The 15 mm is the width the handle is pinched across, not its height off the table, and the handle is the taller dimension. Sweeping it gives a cliff between 10.0 and 11.0 mm of grasp height and a flat plateau from 11 to 15 mm where the screwdriver returns exactly the raspberry's 0/5/9; above 17.5 mm it reaches 0/9/9. The proxy is now 145×15×24 mm, grasp point 12 mm, mid-plateau. This is worth 5 iterative and 9 reflex trials of the 150, and it is the difference between the 68.0% and 84.7% an earlier version of this page reported and the 71.3% and 90.7% here.

Approach conditions. The hardware protocol samples ten approach poses per object with lateral jitter of ±3 cm and exactly one adversarial pose level with the grasp point. The reconstruction used ±150 mm of lateral offset and put the eight elevated conditions at 100 and 250 mm. Lateral jitter is now ±30 mm as stated, and the elevated conditions sit at 150 and 250 mm.

Measuring the support-plane tolerance

An earlier version of this page called the support-plane tolerance "the one fitted parameter" and set it to 10 mm, on the argument that a capsule of radius 7.5–10 mm wrapped around a thin finger link can breach the plane by less than its own radius without the link doing so. That argument is testable, and the number it supports is not 10 mm.

code/calibrate_capsule_envelope.py compares two lowest points of the hand in the same world frame at every one of the {{ENV_N}} hand states the three strategies command across the 15 objects and 10 approach conditions: the lowest point of the 13-capsule proxy, and the lowest point of the model's own collision geometry. A capsule test with tolerance τ and a mesh test with no tolerance agree exactly when τ equals the difference, so the distribution of that difference is the tolerance.

The capsule proxy encloses the collision geometry at every one of the {{ENV_N}} states, so the tolerance is one-sided and the capsule test is never optimistic. The capsule that reaches lowest is a distal phalanx in 96.6% of those states — the thumb tip alone in 79.9% — so the tolerance is close to the difference between an 8–10 mm capsule radius and the fingertip it wraps.

Two checks on the measurement. The gap depends on the hand's configuration and on its wrist pitch but not on its yaw, since the wrist rotation is Rz·Ry and Rz leaves the world vertical fixed; sampling six yaws gives a spread of {{ENV_YAW}}. And MuJoCo collides a mesh as its convex hull, which could make the collision geometry a loose stand-in for the part, so the same measurement was repeated against the undecimated visual meshes: the two agree to a median of {{ENV_VIS}} mm. The collision geometry is the part, and the only meaningful over-approximation in the chain is the capsule proxy.

The primary configuration therefore drops the capsule from the support-plane test altogether and uses the collision geometry directly, with no tolerance. The 10 mm value is 1.5× the p95 of the measured envelope; it is what produced the earlier exact agreement on iterative closure, and it also pushed reflex closure to 90.7% against 86.7% measured.

Where the iterative residual sits

Under the mesh test iterative closure fails 43 of 150 trials, {{FAIL_TABLE_ITER}} of them support-plane collisions. The depth of those collisions is bimodal: 13 of the {{FAIL_TABLE_ITER}} are grazes no deeper than 1.7 mm, at which depth a silicone fingertip deflects rather than the grasp failing, while the upper quartile is past 70 mm. Reflex closure's {{FAIL_TABLE_REFLEX}} collisions have no shallow population at all: the shallowest is 51.5 mm and the median 100.9 mm, a hand that cannot reach the pose rather than one that brushes the table.

§8 GraspGen-X under the same test

A learned grasp generator fits this model without modification. GraspGen-X proposes a 6-DoF hand pose rather than an aperture, and its execution has the same shape as the wrist-fixed strategies: approach the proposed pose with the hand open, then interpolate the descriptor's closure with the wrist held still. It was run over the same 15 object proxies with the released model and the official Inspire-hand descriptor, producing 100 ranked candidates per object.

Two things had to change, and both make the test easier for GGX rather than harder. Its terminal check cannot be the antipodal-midpoint test, which assumes a precision pinch; it uses the descriptor's own declared capture volume instead, so the question is whether the proposal engages the object on GGX's terms. And its descriptor closes all five fingers into an enveloping grasp, so the strict fingertip-only contact rule scores it {{GGX_STRICT}} of 150 by construction. The table below is the primary model configuration, whose enveloping contact rule the three hand-designed strategies also benefit from.

§9 Where the model disagrees with the hardware

On the delicate subset the geometric model puts reflex above iterative, {{DEL_REFLEX}} against {{DEL_ITER}}, while the hardware benchmark puts iterative above reflex, 94% against 80%. The disagreement is expected and locates the boundary of what this analysis explains. Every delicate-object failure the manuscript attributes to reflex closure is a contact-dynamics event, objects skidding or being damaged during the faster single-move closure, and none of those mechanisms exists in a kinematic model.

The result is therefore an operating-regime argument rather than a ranking. Geometry accounts for why naive closure fails and why reflex retains the low-clearance region; it does not account for the delicate-object advantage of iterative closure, which the hold-phase force data has to carry.

Reflex closure is 4.0 points high in aggregate, 90.7% against 86.7%, which is six trials and inside the hardware confidence interval. Iterative closure is the real disagreement at 10.7 points, and §7 locates it in a population of shallow support-plane grazes the model counts as failures. The mechanism that dominates the hardware reflex residual, the thumb backstop slipping during the closing sweep, has no representation here at all.

§10 What this does not settle

Each item names the measurement that would close it.

  • Arm reachability is not modelled. The hand base is commanded directly, so a start pose admitted here may be unreachable for the UR5e. Re-run the sweep with the mink IK from rh56_controller/grasp_viz.py --robot in the loop and report the admissible fraction that survives a UR5e IK solve with joint limits.
  • Object proxies are boxes, cylinders and spheres. Concave and irregular geometry, which is where the manuscript reports most learned-planner failures, cannot be represented. Export the measured meshes with tools/export_paper_object_proxy_meshes.py and repeat with mesh collision.
  • The graze hypothesis is untested. §7 attributes the 14-point iterative shortfall to support-plane contact shallower than a couple of millimetres, which hardware tolerates and the model does not. Testing it needs the per-trial failure cause from the hardware benchmark: if iterative closure's 27 hardware failures are dominated by object displacement rather than by aborted approaches, the hypothesis is wrong. Nothing in the current archive records hardware failure causes.
  • Fingertip compliance is not modelled. The collision geometry is the rigid part. Adding the silicone pad moves the fingertip surface outward by 1–2 mm and makes the model more pessimistic, not less, so it cannot close the gap, but it would change which capsule binds. --radius-scale sweeps the capsule radii and not the mesh.
  • The reflex residual is three trials and cannot be resolved at this sample size. Simulation gives 90.7% against 86.7% measured, inside the hardware 95% confidence interval of 80.2–91.7%. Separating a real 2-point difference from binomial noise needs roughly 1500 trials per strategy, which is not the experiment this benchmark ran.
  • The support surface is the plane z = 0, not the measured table. Registering the real table height would change the low-clearance boundary directly, since every naive failure below {{NAIVE_ZERO_MAX}} mm is set by it.
  • Approach is a straight line to the pre-grasp pose. That is the low-complexity motion-planning assumption the reviewer response named. Replacing it with an L-shaped travel-then-descend move, --approach-path via-overhead, reproduces 48.0%, 71.3% and 90.7% in all 450 trials, so every support-plane collision reported here happens during closure rather than during the free-space transit.
  • Ten approach conditions per object is the hardware protocol, not a convergence-checked sample. The dense grid in the region maps uses {{GRID_N}} start poses per object and can be extended in run_reachability_sweep.py --sampler grid.
